# Proof of Concept: Blank Pixel: The Markless

## High Concept

Blank Pixel: The Markless is a progression-focused adventure RPG about a survivor with no valid identity trying to earn a place in a guarded frontier settlement.

The player begins as one of the Markless: people with no civic record, no trade mark, no guild standing, and no trusted history. They arrive at Hearthmere, a settlement built on the edge of lands slowly being consumed by a strange force called the Hollowing.

Hearthmere does not accept people because they are interesting, heroic, or desperate. It accepts people who can be trusted to help the settlement survive.

The player is allowed inside because an unknown person with influence vouched for them. That person is known in the records only as the Sponsor. The Welcomer's first line frames the entire game:

```text
You are inside the settlement, which means someone important decided you were worth the risk.
```

The central question of the game is:

```text
Were you worth saving?
```

The player answers that question through work, skill progression, trainer approval, reputation, crafting, combat preparation, and mastery.

## Core Fantasy

The player starts with nothing stable.

- No trusted name.
- No legal standing.
- No trade mark.
- No home.
- No proof of who they were.

By playing, the player creates proof.

Every useful item made, every task finished, every trainer mark earned, every recipe discovered, every outpost repaired, and every Hollowed threat defeated becomes part of the player's new identity.

The fantasy is not simply becoming strong. It is becoming impossible to erase.

## Setting

### Hearthmere

Hearthmere is the starting settlement and the player's first safe location.

It is not a peaceful tutorial village. It is a practical, guarded, hard-working place where every resource matters. The settlement exists near unstable borderlands where old roads, ruined outposts, abandoned mines, and forgotten towns have been affected by the Hollowing.

Hearthmere survives because its people are useful to each other.

Walls need repairs. Tools break. Weapons need replacing. Furnaces need ore. Workstations need timber. Guards need equipment. Scouts need supplies. Refugees need screening. Nobody has enough time, materials, or trust to waste.

This makes early gathering and crafting feel like settlement work rather than disconnected tutorial chores.

### The Hollowed Lands

The lands beyond Hearthmere have been damaged by the Hollowing.

The Hollowed Lands should feel familiar but unstable. They are not pure darkness or abstract evil. They are ordinary places that have started losing meaning, structure, and memory.

Examples:

- Roads that no longer lead where old maps say they should.
- Mines whose tunnels shift around forgotten labor.
- Forests where marked trees regrow without rings.
- Ruined houses with tools but no makers.
- Graves with blank stones.
- Weapons that become brittle because nobody remembers who forged them.
- Outposts where banners remain, but the symbols have faded.

The world is not only being destroyed. It is being made unfinished.

## The Hollowing

The Hollowing is the main supernatural threat.

It erases identity, memory, and permanence. It affects people, places, items, records, and even skills.

The Hollowing does not simply kill a town. It makes the town difficult to prove. Its name vanishes from maps. Its people vanish from records. Its tools lose maker marks. Its roads become unreliable. Eventually, the world behaves as if the place was never fully real.

This connects directly to the player.

The player begins as a blank space in the settlement records. The Hollowing creates blank spaces in the world. Some people fear that the player survived because they were touched by it. Others suspect the player is a deserter, criminal, spy, or cursed remnant of an erased expedition.

The truth should be more complicated.

## Player Starting Situation

The player was found outside the safe roads after a disaster.

Recommended starting backstory:

```text
The player was recovered near the remains of a failed expedition route. The expedition was sent beyond Hearthmere's borders to investigate a Hollowed site, recover lost records, or secure rare materials. Most of the expedition vanished from official history. When the player was found, they had no valid papers, no trade mark, and no confirmed witnesses.
```

The player remembers fragments:

- Smoke.
- A broken gate.
- Tools scattered in mud.
- Someone calling names from the dark.
- A symbol they cannot place.
- The feeling of being deliberately left behind or deliberately saved.

Hearthmere cannot tell whether the player is innocent, dangerous, cursed, or useful.

The settlement admits the player under a temporary legal status:

```text
Second Chance Trial
```

Under this status, the player can sleep inside the walls, speak with trainers, receive basic tools, and do supervised settlement work. They cannot freely access advanced contracts, dangerous regions, property, rare recipes, high-tier equipment, or sensitive records until they earn approval.

## The Markless

The Markless are people without recognized identity or useful standing.

A person can become Markless in several ways:

- Exile.
- Criminal sentence.
- Desertion.
- Loss of guild standing.
- Refugee displacement.
- Hollowing exposure.
- Surviving an event that erased official records.

Being Markless does not automatically mean being guilty. It means being unproven.

This is important for tone. The player should not feel like everyone hates them. Instead, they should feel that everyone is carefully measuring the risk of trusting them.

## Marks

Marks are Hearthmere's trust system.

A Mark is part work license, part reputation, part civic identity, and part magical or cultural protection against the Hollowing. Marks prove that a person has completed work, earned witness, and contributed something real.

The player starts with no Marks.

Early Marks:

- Timber Mark: proves the player can gather basic repair and building materials.
- Ore Mark: proves the player can recover raw resources from dangerous places.
- Forge Mark: proves the player can refine raw materials into stable components.
- Craft Mark: proves the player can create useful tools, weapons, or settlement goods.
- Field Mark: proves the player can operate outside the walls.
- Ward Mark: proves the player can resist or help contain Hollowing effects.
- Master Mark: proves deep skill mastery in a discipline.

Marks should have gameplay and story value.

Gameplay uses:

- Unlock trainers.
- Unlock recipes.
- Unlock resource tiers.
- Unlock regions.
- Unlock contracts.
- Unlock reputation ranks.
- Unlock boss preparation steps.

Story uses:

- NPCs use more respectful dialogue.
- The Welcomer updates the player's standing.
- Settlement records begin filling in.
- The player gains permission to investigate their past.
- The Sponsor's hidden interest becomes more visible.

## The Welcomer

The Welcomer is the first major NPC and the face of Hearthmere's approval system.

Despite the friendly title, the Welcomer is an intake officer, trust keeper, settlement official, and quiet judge of character. They process refugees, exiles, recovered survivors, and other uncertain arrivals.

Personality:

- Calm.
- Dry.
- Observant.
- Fair but unsentimental.
- Hard to impress.
- Occasionally funny in a practical, tired way.

The Welcomer does not hate the player. They may actually be one of the first people to treat the player with fairness. But they believe trust is built through finished work.

Gameplay role:

- Introduce the Second Chance Trial.
- Explain approval.
- Direct the player to trainers.
- Track earned Marks.
- Update quest objectives.
- Unlock settlement permissions.
- React to reputation changes.
- Slowly reveal controlled hints about the Sponsor and the player's past.

Continuity note:

The Welcomer should know more than they say, but they should not be secretly evil by default. They are withholding information because Hearthmere is dangerous, the player's status is uncertain, and the Sponsor's involvement is politically sensitive.

## Trainers

Trainers are not just skill teachers. They are settlement officials who can endorse the player.

Each trainer teaches practical work and tests a different kind of trust.

### Woodcutting Trainer

Role:

- First practical task.
- Teaches tool use, gathering, and returning with proof.
- Introduces the rhythm of trainer approval.

Story meaning:

Timber work proves the player can complete a simple useful task. Wood is needed for repairs, handles, fires, storage, barricades, bows, workstations, and daily survival.

Personality:

- Calm.
- Outdoorsy.
- Encouraging.
- Patient.

The task should not frame the game as being about woodcutting. It should frame woodcutting as the first visible way to prove reliability.

### Mining Trainer

Role:

- Teaches resource risk.
- Introduces ore gathering.
- Moves the player from simple labor toward dangerous work.

Story meaning:

Mining proves the player can handle uncomfortable, risky, necessary work. Ore supports tools, weapons, hinges, nails, armor, mechanisms, and settlement defense.

Personality:

- Gruff.
- Practical.
- Blunt.
- Secretly kind.

### Smelting or Forge Trainer

Role:

- Teaches refining.
- Shows that raw materials are incomplete.
- Turns ore into stable crafting components.

Story meaning:

Smelting is the bridge between effort and usefulness. It reinforces the idea that the player must learn to shape raw survival into something the settlement can depend on.

### Crafting or Smithing Trainer

Role:

- Teaches recipes.
- Introduces first gear.
- Connects multiple skills into one useful result.

Story meaning:

Crafting proves the player can create value. The settlement does not only need gathered materials. It needs tools, weapons, repairs, stations, and equipment.

Personality:

- Clever.
- Organized.
- Slightly sarcastic.
- Serious about materials.

### Future Trainers

Possible later trainers:

- Fletching Trainer: bows, arrows, precision, patience.
- Carpentry Trainer: settlement upgrades, storage, workstations, defenses.
- Combat Trainer: survival, discipline, controlled violence.
- Magic Trainer: Hollowing resistance, strange materials, memory-bound craft.
- Archivist: records, lost names, recipe discovery, erased histories.

## Core Progression Loop

Mechanical loop:

```text
Gather -> Refine -> Craft -> Improve -> Prove Mastery
```

Story loop:

```text
Receive limited trust -> Complete useful work -> Return with proof -> Earn a Mark -> Gain responsibility
```

Early trainer loop:

```text
Talk to Welcomer
Get sent to trainer
Receive tool or task
Gather or craft required item
Return to trainer
Trainer marks approval
Return to Welcomer
Unlock next responsibility
```

This structure keeps skills, quests, NPCs, reputation, and story connected.

## Mastery System

Mastery is the long-term identity of the game.

A skill is not mastered by repeating one action forever. Mastery means the player understands a discipline deeply enough to use it across different situations.

The player proves mastery through:

- Normal action XP.
- First-time craft XP.
- Discovery XP.
- Trainer task XP.
- Crafting orders.
- Recipe milestones.
- Skill challenges.
- Quality crafting.
- Rare material use.
- Boss preparation items.
- Masterwork crafts.

This prevents the game from becoming repetitive grinding. Repetition can still exist, but the best progress should come from variety, discovery, useful work, and meaningful goals.

### Why Mastery Matters in the Story

The Hollowing erases weak, copied, forgotten, and unfinished things first.

Poorly made tools break.
Unnamed weapons fade.
Rushed repairs fail.
Copied charms lose meaning.
Records without witnesses become unreliable.

Mastered work resists the Hollowing because it carries proof:

- A maker.
- A purpose.
- A material history.
- A witnessed use.
- A place in the world.

This gives crafting and skill progression a story purpose. The player is not only leveling skills. They are learning how to make things that last in a world that forgets.

### Example: Smithing Mastery

Smithing mastery should not require making hundreds of one item.

A better Smithing path:

- Smelt a basic bar.
- Craft a knife.
- Craft an axe.
- Craft a pickaxe.
- Craft a short sword.
- Complete a trainer order.
- Discover a reinforced tool recipe.
- Craft gear for a settlement guard.
- Use rare ore from a dangerous region.
- Craft a high-quality weapon.
- Create a boss preparation item.
- Pass a Smithing Trial.
- Craft a Masterwork item.

This creates a career arc instead of a grind wall.

## The Sponsor

The Sponsor is the unknown person who got the player admitted into Hearthmere.

The Sponsor should be introduced early only as an absence.

Early hints:

- The Welcomer says someone important decided the player was worth the risk.
- Records refer to the player as "sponsored" but do not name the Sponsor.
- Some NPCs know the word Sponsor but avoid the subject.
- The player finds documents with a name or seal removed.
- Hollowed enemies or locations react strangely to the player.
- The Welcomer refuses to explain more until the player has earned Marks.

The Sponsor should appear later, after the player has earned real trust. The reveal should not be purely good or purely evil.

Best continuity direction:

```text
The Sponsor saved the player, but not only out of kindness. The Sponsor needed a living survivor of the erased expedition because the player may be proof that someone can return from the Hollowing without becoming empty.
```

The Sponsor's plan:

```text
Use the player's earned identity as an anchor to restore erased places, recover lost records, and create permanent defenses against the Hollowing.
```

The dark side:

- The player was not brought to Hearthmere only to recover.
- The player was brought there to be rebuilt.
- Every Mark, skill, reputation rank, and mastery achievement may be making the player a stronger anchor.
- Restoring erased places could also restore old monsters, buried crimes, dead names, and the truth about the expedition.
- The Sponsor may have caused, enabled, or failed to prevent the disaster that made the player Markless.

Recommended Sponsor identity:

The Sponsor should be a morally gray founder, scholar, or former expedition leader connected to Hearthmere's Mark system.

They should believe their plan is necessary. They may genuinely care about the player, but they are still using them.

This makes the twist stronger than "secret villain."

The emotional reveal:

```text
You were saved because you mattered.
You were also saved because you were useful.
```

### Sponsor Reveal Pacing

The Sponsor should not become a named character too early. The early game works better if the player feels the absence of this person before meeting them.

Recommended reveal pacing:

1. Opening: the Welcomer says someone important vouched for the player.
2. Early approvals: records mention that the player is "sponsored" but hide the name.
3. After Timber, Ore, and Craft Marks: the Welcomer admits the Sponsor was connected to the failed expedition.
4. First Hollowed region: the player finds an item, seal, or partial note tied to the Sponsor.
5. Mid game: an Archivist reveals that the Sponsor helped design or formalize Hearthmere's Mark system.
6. Late mid game: the Sponsor contacts the player indirectly through a letter, messenger, dream, ward, or restored record.
7. Act 3: the player meets the Sponsor or uncovers enough evidence to confront them.
8. Act 4: the player decides whether to complete, alter, expose, or reject the Sponsor's plan.

This pacing lets the mystery support the game without overwhelming the early trainer loop.

### Sponsor Continuity Rules

To keep the twist fair, the Sponsor should follow these rules:

- The Sponsor must have a believable reason to save the player.
- The Sponsor must also have a believable reason to stay hidden.
- The Sponsor should not invalidate the player's earned progress.
- The Sponsor's plan should depend on the player's earned Marks and mastery, not replace them.
- The Sponsor should create moral tension, not reveal that all early choices were meaningless.
- The player should eventually have agency over what happens with the Sponsor's plan.

The strongest version is that the Sponsor underestimated the player. They expected to rebuild an anchor. Instead, the player becomes a person with enough earned identity to choose their own purpose.

## Continuity Questions and Proposed Answers

### Why does Hearthmere let the player inside at all?

Because the Sponsor has enough authority or leverage to force a limited admission, but not enough to grant full trust.

This explains why the player can start the game inside the settlement while still being treated with suspicion.

### Why does the Welcomer not name the Sponsor?

Because naming the Sponsor could cause political conflict, panic, or legal consequences. The Sponsor's connection to the failed expedition is sensitive. The Welcomer may also be bound by settlement law until the player earns enough Marks to request access to their own case file.

### Why do trainers give tools to an untrusted person?

The tools are basic, tracked, and part of supervised approval work. Hearthmere needs labor, and the trial exists to turn uncertain people into useful citizens when possible.

### Why does skill progression matter to the main story?

Because mastery creates proof and permanence. Against the Hollowing, strong identity and well-made things have real protective value.

### Why not simply make the player a chosen one?

The player should not be special because destiny says so. The player is unusual because they survived something that should have erased them. What makes them truly important is what they build afterward.

### Is the player guilty of something?

This should remain uncertain early. The player may fear they abandoned someone, opened a gate, carried a cursed object, or survived at someone else's cost. Later reveals can clarify the truth, but the strongest direction is moral complexity rather than simple innocence or simple guilt.

### Is the Sponsor evil?

Not purely. The Sponsor should be dangerous because they are willing to use people for a cause they believe will save many more. They can be loving, guilty, brilliant, manipulative, and necessary at the same time.

### How does this support long-term gameplay?

The story gives meaning to:

- Skill leveling.
- Trainer approvals.
- Reputation.
- Recipe discovery.
- Crafting orders.
- Rare gear.
- Boss preparation.
- Maxing skills.
- Masterwork crafting.
- Restoring regions.
- Finding the player's past.

### Why do Marks resist the Hollowing?

Marks are not just stamps or badges. They are witnessed proof of identity and contribution. A Mark says:

```text
This person did this work.
This work mattered.
Others saw it.
The record holds.
```

The Hollowing weakens things that are forgotten, unfinished, unnamed, or disconnected. Marks resist it because they bind a person to action, memory, community, and record.

This does not mean every Mark is magical in a flashy way. Early Marks can feel civic and practical. Later Marks can reveal that the system has deeper protective value.

### Why does the player need multiple skills?

Because no single skill can create permanence alone.

Wood provides structure.
Ore provides strength.
Smelting provides stability.
Crafting provides purpose.
Combat provides protection.
Reputation provides witness.
Records provide memory.

The best anti-Hollowing work requires multiple systems coming together.

### What should the player know at the start?

The player should know only enough to act.

They should know:

- They are Markless.
- Someone vouched for them.
- Hearthmere is giving them a trial.
- Trainers can help them earn approval.
- The world outside the walls is dangerous.

They should not know yet:

- Who the Sponsor is.
- Whether they caused the expedition disaster.
- Why they survived.
- What the Hollowing truly does.
- That Marks can become anchors against the Hollowing.

This keeps the first hour focused and playable.

## Story Structure

### Act 1: The Second Chance Trial

Purpose:

Teach the core loop and make early skill tasks feel like social proof.

Main beats:

- Player wakes or arrives inside Hearthmere.
- Welcomer explains that someone vouched for them.
- Player receives Second Chance Trial status.
- Player earns Timber Mark.
- Player earns Ore Mark.
- Player learns smelting.
- Player crafts first useful gear.
- Player earns Craft Mark.
- Welcomer grants a Second Chance Badge or equivalent settlement permission.

Emotional arc:

Suspicion becomes cautious acceptance.

### Act 2: Work Beyond the Walls

Purpose:

Expand the world and connect skills to settlement needs.

Main beats:

- Player receives limited permission to leave through controlled routes.
- New resources and recipes open.
- Trainers offer longer approval chains.
- NPC crafting orders begin.
- Player repairs an outpost or reopens a route.
- First Hollowed field threat appears.
- Player learns that their lost expedition may have passed through the same region.

Emotional arc:

Acceptance becomes responsibility.

### Act 3: The Lost Marks

Purpose:

Tie the player's past to the Hollowing and reveal the Sponsor's deeper involvement.

Main beats:

- Player finds erased records related to the expedition.
- An Archivist or Welcomer grants access to partial case files.
- Hollowed enemies react to the player by name or by a forgotten title.
- Player discovers that Marks can anchor memory.
- Sponsor is revealed or directly contacts the player.
- Player learns they were saved because they survived Hollowing exposure.
- Advanced mastery trials begin.

Emotional arc:

Responsibility becomes self-discovery.

### Act 4: Masterwork

Purpose:

Turn mastery into world-changing action.

Main beats:

- Player crafts masterwork gear or settlement anchors.
- Restored regions reveal both hope and buried consequences.
- Bosses guard erased places, lost names, or corrupted Marks.
- Player chooses how to use their identity as an anchor.
- Sponsor's plan is confronted, completed, altered, or rejected.
- Hearthmere recognizes the player as fully marked by earned action, not by birth or paperwork.

Emotional arc:

The player no longer asks whether they were worth saving. They prove they are one of the reasons Hearthmere survives.

### Possible Ending Directions

The proof of concept does not need one final ending yet, but the story should support several strong end states.

Possible endings:

- Restore the erased expedition record and accept the truth of what happened.
- Use the player's mastered identity to anchor a lost region back into the world.
- Reject the Sponsor's plan and protect Hearthmere from becoming another experiment.
- Complete the Sponsor's plan but change its cost.
- Expose the Sponsor and let Hearthmere decide their fate.
- Become Hearthmere's first Markless citizen to earn a Master Mark without birthright, guild inheritance, or official past.

The endings should be based on earned progression, reputation, and key story choices. The player should not lose agency because of the twist.

## Early Game Example Flow

### Opening

The player begins in Hearthmere under watch.

The Welcomer explains:

```text
You are Markless. Not condemned. Not cleared. Markless.
```

The player can ask:

- What happens now?
- How do I earn approval?
- Who brought me here?
- Where should I start?

The Welcomer answers the first three carefully and directs the player to the first trainer.

### Timber Mark

The Woodcutting Trainer gives the player a basic axe or verifies they already have one.

Task:

```text
Collect 5 Normal Logs and return to the Woodcutting Trainer.
```

Story meaning:

The settlement needs repairs and firewood. The trainer is watching whether the player can finish a small responsibility.

Reward:

- Woodcutting XP.
- Timber approval.
- Welcomer acknowledgement.
- Next trainer direction.

### Ore Mark

The Mining Trainer gives the player a basic pickaxe or verifies they already have one.

Task:

```text
Mine Copper Ore and return with proof.
```

Story meaning:

The settlement needs ore for tools and repairs. The trainer is watching whether the player can handle risk.

Reward:

- Mining XP.
- Ore approval.
- Permission to use basic furnace work.

### Forge and Craft Mark

The player smelts Copper Ore into Copper Bar, then crafts a first useful item.

Possible first recipes:

- Knife.
- Bronze Axe.
- Bronze Pickaxe.
- Simple Bow.
- Copper Short Sword.

Story meaning:

The player proves they can turn raw material into useful settlement value.

Reward:

- Smelting XP.
- Crafting or Smithing XP.
- First-time craft XP.
- Craft approval.
- Second Chance Badge.

## Mid Game Systems

After the first approval chain, Hearthmere should open gradually.

Possible systems:

- Crafting orders from NPCs.
- Recipe milestones.
- Region repair projects.
- Trainer approval chains.
- Reputation ranks.
- First rare materials.
- First Hollowed bosses.
- Better tools.
- Settlement upgrades.
- Case file investigation.

Mid game should ask the player to choose priorities:

- Improve tools first.
- Craft better combat gear.
- Unlock a new region.
- Complete trainer approvals.
- Fulfill settlement orders.
- Chase recipe collections.
- Investigate the Sponsor.
- Prepare for a boss.

## Late Game Systems

Late game should focus on mastery and meaningful rare goals.

Possible systems:

- Maxing skills.
- Masterwork crafting.
- Rare gear collection.
- Restoring Hollowed locations.
- Reputation completion.
- Advanced Marks.
- Boss preparation items.
- Memory-bound equipment.
- Prestige cosmetics or titles.
- Final Sponsor decision.

Late game difficulty should come from multi-system requirements, rare ingredients, dangerous regions, boss materials, and high skill checks. It should not come only from huge material counts.

## Tone

The tone should be serious adventure with occasional dry humor.

NPCs should feel like people living in a dangerous world, not tutorial signs. Dialogue should be clear but characterful.

Good tone:

```text
Welcomer: You need their marks showing you can be useful without someone standing over you with a clipboard.
```

```text
Mining Trainer: Swing clean, watch your footing, and do not argue with rocks. They always win eventually.
```

Avoid:

- Long lore dumps at the start.
- NPCs explaining mechanics without world context.
- Making every trainer comic relief.
- Making Hearthmere pointlessly cruel.
- Revealing the Sponsor too early.

## Design Pillars

### 1. Trust Is Earned Through Finished Work

The player gains access by completing useful tasks, not by being declared special.

### 2. Skills Support the Story

Woodcutting, Mining, Smelting, Crafting, Combat, and future skills all help the settlement survive and help the player build identity.

### 3. Mastery Means Breadth and Proof

The player should progress through varied actions: discoveries, first crafts, trainer tasks, orders, milestones, challenges, rare materials, and masterwork goals.

### 4. The World Forgets, But Good Work Lasts

The Hollowing gives a fantasy reason for crafting, records, reputation, and mastery to matter.

### 5. The Sponsor Is Complicated

The person who saved the player should be both protector and manipulator. Their plan should be understandable, useful, and dangerous.

## Implementation Direction

This concept fits the current project direction.

Existing systems already aligned:

- Welcomer approval gate.
- Trainer task flow.
- Woodcutting quest state.
- Mining and crafting trainer setup.
- Resource nodes with skill requirements.
- Inventory item flow.
- Skill XP and level display.
- Quest UI direction.

Recommended next design additions:

1. Define the Second Chance Trial as the official early quest arc.
2. Rename or frame early approvals as Marks.
3. Add Ore Mark and Craft Mark quest states after Timber Mark.
4. Add first-time craft XP tracking.
5. Add basic crafting recipes for the first complete chain.
6. Add Welcomer dialogue that references the unnamed Sponsor.
7. Add a case-file or record hint that the Sponsor exists but is hidden.
8. Add later-game design notes for the Hollowing and Master Marks.

## Unknowns To Decide Later

These details do not need to be solved before implementation starts, but they should be tracked.

1. What was the exact expedition objective?
2. Did the player make a morally questionable choice during the disaster?
3. Is the Sponsor physically present in Hearthmere, missing, exiled, or operating from a hidden location?
4. Are Marks purely cultural at first, or visibly magical from the beginning?
5. What is the player's first direct Hollowing encounter?
6. How many approval Marks are required before leaving the tutorial area?
7. Does the player eventually recover their old name, choose a new name, or reject names as proof?
8. Should the Sponsor be redeemable, condemnable, or dependent on player choices?
9. What is the first boss tied to: a mine, forest, ruined outpost, erased road, or failed expedition site?
10. How much settlement building should be mechanical versus story-based?

Recommended answers for the first build:

- Keep the expedition details vague.
- Keep the Sponsor unnamed.
- Treat Marks as civic approval first, with deeper meaning hinted later.
- Require Timber, Ore, and Craft approvals for the first major milestone.
- Make the first Hollowing clue environmental rather than a full lore explanation.

## One-Sentence Pitch

Blank Pixel: The Markless is a skill-driven adventure RPG where a nameless survivor earns trust in a frontier settlement, masters practical crafts to resist a world-erasing force, and discovers that the mysterious person who saved them may also be using them as the key to a dangerous plan.

## Final Continuity Check

The concept holds together because each major system supports the same theme.

- The player is Markless, so approval matters.
- Hearthmere is resource-strained, so starter tasks matter.
- Trainers are officials, so skill quests matter.
- Marks prove useful work, so reputation matters.
- The Hollowing erases identity, so records and names matter.
- Mastery creates lasting proof, so long-term skill progression matters.
- The Sponsor saved the player for a reason, so the main mystery matters.

The result is a game where skilling, crafting, quests, reputation, and story all point toward one clear fantasy:

```text
You begin as a blank person in a world that forgets.
Through mastery, work, and trust, you become someone impossible to erase.
```
